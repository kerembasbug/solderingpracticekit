Walk into the solder aisle and the choices multiply fast: tin-lead ratios, diameters, flux cores, lead-free alloys. For electronics, though, the decision is simpler than it looks. Here is everything you actually need to know to buy the right spool.

## The short answer

If you want one spool for learning and hobby electronics, buy **0.8 mm (0.031") 63/37 or 60/40 rosin-core solder**. Add a thinner 0.5–0.6 mm spool for surface-mount work, and a lead-free spool when your projects call for it.

## Leaded vs lead-free

The first fork in the road. **Leaded** solder — usually 60/40 or 63/37 tin-lead — melts at a lower temperature (around 183–190°C) and flows beautifully, which makes it the easiest to learn on. **Lead-free** solder (typically SAC alloys with tin, silver and copper) melts hotter (about 217–227°C) and is stickier, but it is required for anything sold commercially or handled by children.

Our full breakdown lives in the [lead-free vs leaded guide](/guides/lead-free-vs-leaded-solder); the short version is **learn on leaded, switch to lead-free when you need to.** A spool of [60/40 rosin-core wire](/reviews/hgmzzq-60-40-rosin-core-solder) is the ideal first purchase.

## Types of solder wire

| Type | Example alloy | Melting point | Use it for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Eutectic leaded** | Sn63/Pb37 | 183°C (361°F) | The most forgiving choice for hobby electronics |
| **Standard leaded** | Sn60/Pb40 | 183–190°C (361–374°F) | Learning, repairs, general electronics |
| **Silver-bearing leaded** | Sn62/Pb36/Ag2 | about 179°C (354°F) | Silver-plated parts and terminals |
| **Lead-free SAC** | SAC305 (Sn96.5/Ag3/Cu0.5) | 217–220°C (423–428°F) | Commercial and child-safe electronics |
| **Lead-free tin-copper** | Sn99.3/Cu0.7 | about 227°C (441°F) | Budget lead-free, larger joints |
| **Low-temperature** | Sn42/Bi58 | 138°C (280°F) | Heat-sensitive parts; brittle, niche use |
| **Acid-core / plumbing** | Various | Varies | Plumbing and metalwork only — **never electronics** |

## 60/40 vs 63/37

Among leaded solders you will see two ratios:

- **60/40** has a small "plastic range" — a few degrees where it is half-set as it cools. Disturb a joint in that window and you can get a dull, weak [cold joint](/guides/how-to-fix-a-cold-solder-joint).
- **63/37** is *eutectic*: it changes straight from liquid to solid with no pasty phase. That makes it slightly more forgiving and the choice of many professionals.

Both are great. If you can find 63/37 easily, take it; if not, 60/40 is perfectly good.

## Solder wire gauge chart

Solder "gauge" just means diameter. Listings mix millimetres, inches and wire gauge numbers, so here is how they line up:

| Diameter (mm) | Inches | Approx. AWG | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.3 mm | 0.012" | 28 AWG | Very fine-pitch SMD and rework |
| 0.5 mm | 0.020" | 24 AWG | Surface-mount parts, fine through-hole |
| 0.6 mm | 0.024" | 22–23 AWG | Fine or dense electronics, small SMD |
| **0.8 mm** | **0.031"** | **20 AWG** | **The all-rounder — most through-hole work** |
| 1.0 mm | 0.039" | 18 AWG | Larger joints, wires, connectors |
| 1.2 mm | 0.047" | 16–17 AWG | Thick wires, terminals |
| 1.6 mm | 0.063" | 14 AWG | Heavy wire, battery and power leads |

AWG figures are approximate and are there to help you compare listings; some sellers use SWG instead, where 0.8 mm is roughly 21 SWG.

## Choosing a diameter

Thinner wire gives you finer control over how much solder you feed; thicker wire delivers more, faster.

- **Too thick** and you flood small pads with solder, causing bridges and blobs.
- **Too thin** and you spend ages feeding solder into a large joint while the tip cools.

If you buy one spool, make it **0.8 mm** — it handles the vast majority of hobby joints comfortably. Match the solder to the job the same way you match the [iron temperature](/guides/soldering-iron-temperature-guide).

## Flux core: type and size

Almost all electronics solder is **flux-core** (often called rosin-core): the wire has one or more channels of flux running through it. As the solder melts, the flux is released exactly where it is needed to clean the joint.

**Flux type** is usually printed on the label:

- **R (rosin)** — mild, traditional rosin flux.
- **RMA (rosin mildly activated)** — a little more cleaning power; a popular hobby choice.
- **RA (rosin activated)** — stronger cleaning for oxidized parts; clean the residue off afterwards.
- **No-clean** — leaves minimal, non-corrosive residue that can usually stay on the board.
- **Water-soluble** — very active, but the residue must be washed off promptly.

**Flux percentage** is the share of the wire's weight that is flux — commonly around 1–3%. Around 2% is a good all-round figure. Some wire has several separate cores ("multi-core") to spread the flux evenly.

Never use acid-core or plumbing solder on electronics — the acid flux corrodes copper and ruins boards. For more on flux, see our [how to use flux guide](/guides/how-to-use-flux).

## How to read a solder label

A typical spool might read: **Sn63Pb37 · 0.8 mm · 2.2% · RMA · 100 g**

- **Sn63Pb37** — 63% tin, 37% lead (Sn is tin, Pb is lead, Ag is silver, Cu is copper, Bi is bismuth).
- **0.8 mm** — the diameter.
- **2.2%** — the flux content by weight.
- **RMA** — the flux type.
- **100 g** — the weight of solder on the spool.

## How much to buy

A 50–100 g spool lasts a beginner a long time. Buy a small spool of leaded 0.8 mm to learn on, and add a thinner 0.5–0.6 mm spool and a lead-free spool later as your projects demand. Pair it with good [flux](/reviews/towot-solder-wick-flux-kit) and a [temperature-controlled iron](/best-soldering-irons), and you have everything the metal side of soldering requires.

## Storage and safety

Keep solder in a dry place and away from food. Leaded solder is safe to handle with sensible habits — wash your hands afterwards, do not eat at the bench, and ventilate the [flux fumes](/guides/soldering-fume-safety), which are the real irritant whichever alloy you use.
