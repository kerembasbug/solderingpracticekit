One of the first choices a new solderer faces is which solder to buy. The short answer: **for learning, leaded is easier; for safety and compliance, lead-free wins.** This guide covers the practical differences — melting points, iron settings, how to hand-solder lead-free well, and how to tell the two apart.

## What's the difference?

- **Leaded solder** is usually a 60/40 or 63/37 tin-lead alloy. It melts at a low temperature, flows beautifully and forms bright, shiny joints — which also makes it easy to *see* when a joint is good.
- **Lead-free solder** is typically a SAC alloy (tin, silver, copper) or a cheaper tin-copper alloy. It melts hotter, flows less eagerly and tends to leave a duller, satin-looking joint even when it is perfectly sound.

## Melting points and iron temperatures

| Alloy | Type | Melting point | Typical iron setting |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sn63/Pb37 (63/37) | Leaded, eutectic | 183°C (361°F) | 315–340°C (600–650°F) |
| Sn60/Pb40 (60/40) | Leaded | 183–190°C (361–374°F) | 315–340°C (600–650°F) |
| SAC305 (Sn96.5/Ag3/Cu0.5) | Lead-free | 217–220°C (423–428°F) | 350–370°C (660–700°F) |
| Sn99.3/Cu0.7 | Lead-free | about 227°C (441°F) | 360–380°C (680–715°F) |

Lead-free melts roughly 35–45°C higher, which is why you run the iron hotter. The full chart by job is in our [soldering iron temperature guide](/guides/soldering-iron-temperature-guide).

## Ease of use compared

| | Leaded | Lead-free |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Flow / wetting | Excellent | Good, needs more flux |
| Joint appearance | Shiny | Often satin or dull (normal) |
| Working window | Forgiving | Narrower — cools faster |
| Tip wear | Low | Higher |
| Ease for beginners | Easier | Harder |
| Cost | Lower | Higher, especially silver alloys |

Because lead-free runs hotter and wets less readily, it punishes weak technique more. That is the main reason it is harder to learn on — and why a good [temperature-controlled station](/best-soldering-irons) helps so much with it.

## How to hand-solder with lead-free solder

Lead-free is entirely manageable by hand with a few adjustments:

1. **Use a slightly bigger tip.** More tip contact moves heat faster, which is better than simply turning the temperature up.
2. **Set around 350–370°C** for ordinary through-hole joints and increase only for large pads or thick wire.
3. **Add flux.** Lead-free wets less readily than leaded, so a touch of extra [flux](/guides/how-to-use-flux) makes a noticeable difference, especially on older or oxidised parts.
4. **Heat the joint, then feed solder into it** — the same technique as leaded, but expect it to take a second or so longer to flow.
5. **Keep the tip tinned.** High-tin lead-free alloys are harder on tips, so re-tin before parking the iron and clean it often. A dry, black tip is the fastest way to make lead-free miserable.
6. **Judge by shape, not shine.** A good lead-free joint is a smooth, concave fillet that has wetted both the pad and the lead. A satin finish is normal; a ball sitting on the pad is not — see our [cold solder joint guide](/guides/how-to-fix-a-cold-solder-joint).

If you are learning specifically on lead-free — for school or training, or because children will handle the work — practise these habits on a cheap [practice board](/best-soldering-practice-kits) before a project you care about.

## How to tell leaded and lead-free solder apart

- **Read the label.** Alloy codes tell you directly: *Sn63Pb37* or *Sn60Pb40* (Pb is lead) means leaded; *SAC305*, *Sn96.5Ag3Cu0.5* or *Sn99.3Cu0.7* means lead-free. Many lead-free products also carry a RoHS mark.
- **Watch it melt.** Leaded solder melts noticeably more easily at the same iron temperature.
- **Look at the joint.** Leaded joints are bright and mirror-like; lead-free joints are usually satin.
- **Check the product.** Most consumer electronics made for the EU and many other markets since the mid-2000s use lead-free solder, which matters when you repair them.

## Mixing leaded and lead-free

Avoid mixing the two on the same joint where reliability or compliance matters — the resulting alloy is no longer lead-free and its properties are unpredictable. In hobby repair, one mix is common and useful: adding a little leaded solder to an old lead-free joint lowers its melting point and makes it much easier to remove with a [solder sucker or wick](/solder-sucker-vs-solder-wick). Just remember the finished repair is no longer lead-free.

## Safety and regulations

Lead is toxic, so the obvious advantage of lead-free is **no lead exposure** — relevant for children, anything handled by others and products sold commercially. The **RoHS** directive requires lead-free solder in most consumer electronics sold in the EU and many other markets.

With leaded solder, the realistic exposure route is **hand-to-mouth**: residue on your fingers ending up on food or your face. Wash your hands after soldering, do not eat at the bench and keep solder away from children. Separately, *both* solders release flux fumes when heated; those fumes are what you breathe at the bench, so [ventilation and fume extraction](/guides/soldering-fume-safety) matter no matter which you choose.

## So which should you buy?

- **Learning at home, by yourself?** Leaded 63/37 or 60/40 (0.6–0.8 mm) is the gentlest way to build technique. Wash your hands and ventilate. Our pick is a spool of [60/40 rosin-core solder](/reviews/hgmzzq-60-40-rosin-core-solder).
- **Soldering with or for [kids and teens](/guides/best-soldering-kits-for-kids-and-teens), or building something others will handle?** Choose lead-free.
- **Building products to sell, or in a RoHS region?** Lead-free is required.

Whatever you pick, get **thin, rosin-core** solder so flux is built in — our [solder wire guide](/guides/solder-wire-types-and-gauge) explains the diameters and alloy codes — and pair it with the right [iron](/guides/how-to-choose-a-soldering-iron) and a [practice kit](/best-soldering-practice-kits).
